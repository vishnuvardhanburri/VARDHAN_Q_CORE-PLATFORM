# Target-Market Portfolio — Broaden the Pipeline, Keep the Offer Focused

## Executive decision

**Do not make LGT the whole go-to-market plan.** Keep LGT as one target-account hypothesis and maintain a wider, evidence-led account portfolio.

The discipline is:

- **Narrow entry problem:** determine whether a defined high-consequence workflow needs stronger provenance, tamper/replay rejection, or independently verifiable trust evidence.
- **Broader market:** test that problem across regulated finance, financial-market infrastructure, communications/critical digital infrastructure, insurance, and selected public digital services.
- **One gated commercial path per qualified buyer:** £15K discovery → £60K proof of value → £500K production. Each transition requires buyer confirmation, technical evidence, and a signed agreement.

The list below is a research queue, not a claim that any organisation has a problem, budget, active programme, or interest in VARDHAN Q-CORE.

## Entry offer to test

Working description: **Verified Workflow Evidence Assessment**.

Take one buyer-selected workflow and answer:

1. What systems create, transmit, authorize, and consume the workflow's important events or records?
2. Where can provenance, authenticity, replay prevention, tamper detection, or audit evidence fail or become hard to verify?
3. What measurable control or operational gap does that create, and what is the buyer's current workaround?
4. Could Q-CORE credibly test one bounded improvement in the buyer's environment? What integrations, evidence, remediation, and assurance would production require?
5. Is there enough verified value, access, sponsorship, and budget path to justify a paid PoV?

The £15K discovery should produce a current-state workflow/trust-boundary map, prioritized findings, feasibility and dependency assessment, an evidence plan, and a go/no-go PoV scope. This is a proposal hypothesis; do not represent outcomes as already proven.

**Do not lead every conversation with “post-quantum cryptography.”** Treat PQC migration, crypto-agility, signed receipts, replay rejection, and audit verification as potential angles to validate against the buyer's stated problem. The first conversation is about the workflow and its consequences, not a feature catalogue.

### How to use this list

This is an initial **research portfolio of 15 candidates**, not a claim that 15 are qualified leads. Expand or replace candidates based on real signals and access. Do not send the same generic message to all accounts.

For each candidate, record:

- Official source and dated public trigger, if one exists.
- Relevant buyer role and a credible route to reach them.
- The exact workflow-level question to ask.
- Buyer-stated pain, current workaround, operational/compliance impact, and priority.
- Sponsor, technical evaluator, procurement route, access feasibility, timing, and budget path.
- Next action, owner, evidence link, and last-contact date.

Useful first roles vary by account: CISO/security architecture, enterprise/platform architecture, cryptography or key-management lead, transaction/platform engineering, operational resilience, or a business owner accountable for the workflow. Select the role based on the question, not seniority alone.

## Qualification and prioritization

Score each account from 0–2 on each criterion (maximum 10):

1. **Problem signal:** public evidence or direct buyer statement indicates a relevant issue; a generic industry trend alone scores no more than 1.
2. **Workflow clarity:** a single workflow and measurable failure mode/control objective can be named.
3. **Buyer access:** there is a credible path to a relevant technical or business owner.
4. **Urgency and impact:** the buyer states why the issue matters now and describes the current cost, risk, or workaround.
5. **Commercial path:** sponsor, feasible access, procurement route, and a plausible paid-discovery budget path are identified.

Treat an account as **qualified for a £15K discovery proposal only after direct buyer validation**, even if the desk-research score is high. Unknowns stay UNKNOWN. A high score is prioritization, not proof of willingness to pay.

## First market-test cadence

Use this as an operating experiment, not a revenue forecast:

1. Research all 15 candidates and identify up to two relevant roles per account.
2. Rank the best five using public, dated evidence and the likelihood of reaching an owner.
3. Send individualized problem questions to that first cohort; do not lead with a £15K proposal or unsupported production claims.
4. Record actual replies and conversations, including “no priority” and “not a fit.”
5. After the first five meaningful buyer conversations—or after the first cohort produces no meaningful signal—review which sector, role, and wording perform best before expanding or changing the offer.
6. Send a £15K proposal only when the buyer confirms a worthwhile problem and a plausible scope, sponsor, access route, and buying path.

The most useful near-term output is not a large list of cold contacts. It is **one buyer-confirmed problem with a named owner, measurable impact, feasible access, and a credible paid-discovery route**, supported by enough additional candidates that one unanswered account cannot stall the plan.

## Commercial truth and stop rules

- An account on this list is a target, not a lead; a lead is not qualified; a proposal is not a contract; a contract is not collected cash.
- Do not imply that a listed organization uses Q-CORE, has an active need, or has evaluated it.
- Do not claim production readiness, certification, independent security assessment, or customer-proven ROI without matching evidence.
- Do not expand product scope to satisfy every sector. Keep the first test to one buyer-owned workflow and one agreed acceptance plan.
- Stop or reposition if repeated buyer conversations fail to establish material pain, technical feasibility, access, a sponsor, or a commercial path.
